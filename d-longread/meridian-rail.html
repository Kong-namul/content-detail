Meridian Rail

Redesigning how forty million passengers find their way through the station

HERO VIDEO · 16:9

Meridian Rail runs the lines that stitch together the north of England, and its flagship hub, Meridian Central, sees forty million journeys a year. It is a Victorian shed grafted onto three decades of extensions: twelve platforms, four concourses, two shopping arcades and a tram stop that most people discover by accident.

The signs had grown the same way. Every owner of every extension had added their own, in their own typeface, at their own height. Passengers stopped reading and started asking, and the information desk fielded more questions about where to walk than about trains. Missed connections cost the railway money and cost travellers their patience.

Meridian asked Ondo Studio for something modest on paper: a new wayfinding system for the station. We suggested a larger question. What would it take for a first-time visitor to cross the building without breaking stride, without a map, and without feeling lost for even a minute?

The opportunity

Before drawing anything, we spent three weeks on site. We counted how many times people stopped, turned a full circle or checked a phone, and marked each hesitation on a floor plan. The map that came back looked like a weather chart, with storms gathering at the foot of every staircase and in front of every pair of doors.

That became the opportunity. The station did not need more information, it needed the right piece at the right moment. Someone choosing a direction needs one word and an arrow. Someone who has chosen needs reassurance, and someone who is late needs the quickest line through the crowd.

PHOTO · 16:9

Listening on the platform

We rode early trains with commuters, stood at the barriers through the evening peak, and sat with the information desk team for a full shift. We asked people to draw the station from memory. Almost nobody drew the same building twice, but nearly everyone drew the same three places they were afraid of getting lost.

Accessibility groups walked the concourses with us, and they changed the brief more than anyone. A short route is not always a step-free one, and a sign hung at head height disappears behind a crowd or from a wheelchair. We designed for the hardest journey first and let the easy ones follow.

Out of this came a plain principle we repeated on every wall of the studio: say where you are, say where to go next, and say it once. Anything beyond that was noise, however beautifully set. It also gave the client team a test they could apply long after we had left the building.

A station that explains itself

The system gives every part of the station a name and a colour you can read from across the hall. Platforms, concourses and exits fall into five districts, each marked by a single word. Walk toward the word you recognise, and the next sign appears just before you start to doubt.

The lettering is set large but kept quiet against the architecture, so the iron roof still has room to breathe. At decision points, signs hang lower and carry pictograms drawn from the station’s own ironwork. Step-free routes follow a thin rule along the floor that you can trace without looking up.

SIGNAGE · 16:9

Making it real

We never trust a sign until it has been ignored in public. For eight weeks we taped paper versions across the east concourse, moved them at night, and watched what happened by day. Some of our favourites failed within the hour, and a few we had nearly cut turned out to be the busiest in the building.

The pilot then moved to full-size signs on one concourse and two platforms, running six months through timetable changes, a marathon weekend and a rail strike. We revised the wording every fortnight, and the station team learned to reprint a panel before lunch. By the end, the pilot had taught us more about the building than the drawings ever had.

What we delivered

  • A naming system of five colour-coded districts
  • A wayfinding typeface and a 120-symbol pictogram set
  • Hanging, wall-mounted and floor-level sign families
  • Step-free route language with tactile floor strips
  • Departure boards and a mobile wayfinding page
  • A maintenance guide and print kit for station staff

The impact

Over the six-month pilot, wayfinding questions at the information desk fell by 41 percent, freeing staff for the harder cases. Average interchange time between platforms dropped by two minutes, which on a crowded Friday evening is the difference between the 17:42 and the 18:12.

Passenger ratings for finding the way around rose from 58 to 81 out of 100, and the rollout to the network’s other twelve stations begins next spring. Station teams now maintain the system themselves, using the kit we left behind, and report that new signs take days to commission rather than months.
